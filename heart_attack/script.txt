Section one. The clock in your chest.
Here's what's actually happening during a heart attack. Your heart is a muscle, and like every muscle, it needs a steady supply of oxygen-rich blood. That blood arrives through small arteries wrapped around the outside of the heart, called the coronary arteries.
Over the years, fatty plaque can build up inside those arteries. Most of the time, it just sits there. But sometimes a plaque cracks open, and your body treats that crack like a wound. Platelets rush in. A clot forms. And suddenly, a pipe that was carrying blood to part of your heart is blocked.
Think of a garden hose feeding a flower bed. Kink the hose, and the flowers don't die the instant the water stops. They wilt. Slowly at first, then faster. Heart muscle is the same. The longer the blockage lasts, the more muscle is lost, and muscle that dies doesn't grow back.
Cardiologists have a phrase for this. Time is muscle.
And the research makes that phrase very real. In 2004, researchers published a study in the journal Circulation that followed close to eighteen hundred heart attack patients treated with emergency angioplasty, the procedure that opens the blocked artery with a tiny balloon. They found that every thirty-minute delay in treatment was linked with about a seven and a half percent higher relative risk of dying within the next year.
Every thirty minutes. Not every day. Not every few hours.
Now think about what saved heart muscle actually means in your life. It's the strength to climb the stairs to your bedroom. It's walking to the mailbox without stopping to catch your breath. It's not needing oxygen tubing in your living room. The muscle you save in the first hour is the muscle you'll be living on for the next twenty years.
So the real question isn't whether you'll get to the hospital. It's how fast. And that starts with recognizing what's happening, which is harder than most people think.

Section four. Action number two. Chew an aspirin, the right way.
Once 911 is on the way, the next thing to think about is aspirin. And this is one of the most powerful, least expensive tools in all of emergency medicine.
Remember that clot forming at the cracked plaque? It's built largely by platelets, tiny cells in your blood that clump together to stop bleeding. Aspirin makes those platelets less sticky. It doesn't dissolve the clot that's already there, but it helps stop it from growing bigger while help is on the way.
The evidence here goes back decades. In 1988, a landmark trial called ISIS-2 was published in The Lancet. It included more than seventeen thousand patients with a suspected heart attack. Those given aspirin early had about a twenty-three percent lower risk of dying from vascular causes over the next five weeks. And when aspirin was combined with a clot-busting medication, the reduction was about forty-two percent.
Twenty-three percent. From one small tablet.
But how you take it matters. Current American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology guidelines call for regular, non-coated aspirin, usually one full-strength three hundred twenty-five milligram tablet, or four low-dose eighty-one milligram tablets. And you chew it. Don't swallow it whole. Chewing gets it into your bloodstream faster. Coated aspirin is designed to dissolve slowly, which is the opposite of what you want in this moment.
Now, here's the part that is just as important as the aspirin itself. Aspirin is not safe for everyone.
If you're allergic to aspirin or similar pain relievers, don't take it. If you have active bleeding, a recent stomach bleed, a recent bleed in the brain, or a bleeding disorder, don't take it. And if you're on a blood thinner, like warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, or clopidogrel, tell the 911 dispatcher and let them guide you.
And this one could save a life all by itself. Aspirin is for heart attack symptoms, not stroke symptoms. If someone's face is drooping, if one arm is weak, or if their speech is slurred, that could be a stroke. And some strokes are caused by bleeding in the brain, where aspirin could make things worse. Section five. Action number three. Stop, sit down, and use your own nitroglycerin.
The third action sounds simple, but almost nobody does it. Stop what you're doing and sit down.

Section seven. If someone collapses, your hands and an AED.
Now I want to talk to the people who love you, because sometimes the person having the heart attack can't help themselves.
A heart attack can trigger something even more dangerous called cardiac arrest. That's when the heart's electrical system goes haywire and the heart stops pumping effectively. The person collapses, stops responding, and isn't breathing normally. Without help, the brain starts running out of oxygen within minutes.
Here's what to do. Call 911, or tell someone else to call. Then start hands-only CPR. Put the heel of one hand in the center of the chest, your other hand on top, and push hard and fast. At least two inches deep, about one hundred to one hundred twenty times a minute. That's roughly the beat of the song "Stayin' Alive." Don't stop until help takes over.
Does it really make a difference? A study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2015 looked at more than thirty thousand cardiac arrests outside the hospital in Sweden. When a bystander started CPR before the ambulance arrived, thirty-day survival was about ten and a half percent. Without it, it was about four percent.
More than two and a half times better. From two hands.
