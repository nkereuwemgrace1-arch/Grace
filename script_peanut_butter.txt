Section four. The two o'clock crash, and how to stop it.

Section six. Your bones need more than calcium.
When people think about bones, they think calcium and vitamin D. Fair enough. But two other minerals get almost no attention, and peanut butter delivers both.
The first is phosphorus. Calcium doesn't build bone by itself. It partners with phosphorus to form the hard mineral crystals that give your skeleton its strength.
The second is magnesium, and this one surprises people. Your body needs magnesium to activate vitamin D. If your magnesium is low, the vitamin D you're taking may not be doing its full job. I've had patients faithfully swallowing vitamin D pills for years, puzzled about why their bone scans weren't improving.
The research supports this. A study drawn from the Framingham Heart Study, published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, found that older men and women with higher magnesium and potassium intakes had greater bone mineral density.

Section eight. How to do this the right way.
You've stayed with me this long, so let's make sure you walk away with a plan that actually works. Because the wrong jar, eaten the wrong way, can cancel out almost everything I've just described.
Rule one. Turn the jar around before it goes in your cart.
Read the ingredients. The best peanut butter has one or two. Peanuts, and maybe salt. That's it.
If you see hydrogenated oil, sugar, corn syrup, or a long list of words you can't pronounce, put it back. Those oils are added so the jar never separates, and they usually travel with added sugar that works against everything we talked about for your blood sugar. Check the sodium, too. Choose a jar with little or no added salt, especially if you watch your blood pressure.
Natural peanut butter will separate, with oil pooling on top. That's not a defect. That's honesty. Stir it well once, then keep it in the refrigerator so it stays mixed and fresh.
Rule two. Two tablespoons is your daily amount.
That's about one hundred ninety calories, seven to eight grams of protein, and a solid dose of the nutrients we've covered. Use a real measuring spoon for the first week. Most people are shocked by how big their idea of a tablespoon really is.
Rule three. Swap, don't stack.
Peanut butter should replace something, not add to everything. Replace the cookies. Replace the crackers and cheese. Replace the jam on your toast. That's how you get the appetite benefit without the weight gain.
Rule four. Pair it wisely.
Peanut butter on white bread with a glass of juice is a sugar rush in disguise. Peanut butter on whole-grain toast, stirred into oatmeal, spread on apple slices, or mixed into plain Greek yogurt is a completely different story. If your goal is steady energy, have it at breakfast. If your goal is muscle, have it within a couple of hours of a walk or some light exercise.
Rule five. Know when to be careful.
If you have a peanut allergy, this food isn't for you, and that's perfectly fine. Other nuts and seeds offer many of the same benefits.
If you have kidney disease, talk to your doctor first, because peanut butter contains potassium and phosphorus, which you may need to limit.
And this one is important for many people my patients' age. If you have any trouble swallowing, thick peanut butter can be a real choking hazard. Spread it thin, stir it into warm oatmeal or a smoothie, and never eat it by the heaping spoonful on its own.
Rule six. Be patient. Be consistent.
None of this happens from one sandwich. These benefits build over weeks and months. Give it ninety days. Two tablespoons. The right jar. The right swaps. Then ask your doctor to check your cholesterol and blood sugar at your next visit, and see what's changed.
